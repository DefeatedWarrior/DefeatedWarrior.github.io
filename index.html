Problem Statement

Young Australians working ordinary jobs like teaching, nursing and retail can no longer afford to buy a home in Canberra. Since 2000, house prices have risen more than twice as fast as wages, creating a structural gap that individual saving habits cannot bridge. A home that used to cost around three times an average annual salary now costs over ten times that amount. This intergenerational inequity means an entire generation is bearing the consequences of policy decisions, including negative gearing, capital gains tax discounts, and underinvestment in housing supply, that were made long before they entered the workforce. This data story examines the evidence behind that claim.

Section 01: Context

What happened to the Australian Dream?

For most of the last century, buying a home was just a normal part of life in Australia. Today it has become one of the biggest financial challenges young Australians face.

For most of the 20th century, housing in Australia functioned primarily as shelter. Prices were tied closely to construction costs, local incomes, and population growth. That relationship began to break down in the late 1990s when a combination of policy decisions fundamentally changed what housing was for. Negative gearing, which allows investors to deduct rental losses against their income tax, and the 1999 capital gains tax discount made property one of the most tax-advantaged investment classes in Australia. This accelerated the commodification of housing: the process by which a basic necessity is increasingly treated as a financial asset rather than a social one. Record-low interest rates and restricted supply added further pressure. Between 2000 and 2024, the Canberra median house price rose from $191,511 to over $980,000, an increase of more than 400%. The average full-time wage grew from $38,000 to $100,016 over the same period, which is less than a third of that rate (ABS, 2025; CoreLogic, 2025).

Between 1980 and 2024, house prices in Australia grew by 1,599%. Wages grew by just 540% over the same period. (Sources: BIS/FRED QAUN628BIS; ABS Average Weekly Earnings 6302.0)

The consequences of this market failure are not shared equally. The compounding effect of rising property values means those who already own homes accumulate wealth passively, while those who don't fall further behind with each passing year. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality. Essential workers, including nurses, teachers, and childcare workers, are being priced out of the very communities they serve, which threatens the functioning of public services as much as it threatens individual wellbeing. ABS data shows home ownership among 25–29 year olds has fallen from 50% in 1971 to just 36% in 2021 (ABS, 2021). More than half of low-income renter households now spend over 30% of their income on housing (AHURI, 2024), money that cannot go toward savings, education, or economic participation. When a basic need becomes unaffordable for ordinary workers, it stops being a personal problem and becomes a public one.

Section 03: The Human Story
"It should be a basic human right, not a privilege, not an investment. It’s something you need. And right now, people have twenty houses they rent out, while other people can’t even buy one."
Mum, Canberra, interviewed June 2026

What she told me

Mum grew up in an immigrant family that arrived in Australia with nothing. For them, owning a home represented economic security and social belonging, the tangible proof that hard work could produce a stable life. Her parents bought a three-bedroom house in Canberra for $156,000, roughly three times the average annual wage at the time. She noted that the relationship between effort and reward that made homeownership achievable for her parents no longer exists in the same way. The same house now costs over $900,000. When I asked whether young people have a fair shot at buying a home today, she was direct: “You’ve got zero chance, Will, unless Mum and Dad help you out.” She rejected the idea that this is about individual spending discipline, recognising instead that it reflects a structural mismatch between the price of housing and the wages most people earn, one that cannot be solved through personal behaviour alone.

What the data backs up

The data confirms what Mum described. In 2000, the Canberra median house price was $191,511, approximately five times the average annual wage, already stretched compared to the 1985 ratio of around four times. By 2025 it had reached $1,040,000, now over ten times the average wage (ABS, 2025; CoreLogic, 2025). This is the compounding effect of commodification: as each generation of owners benefits from rising values, the barrier for the next generation rises with it. ABS Census data shows home ownership for 25–29 year olds has dropped from 50% in 1971 to just 36% in 2021 (ABS, 2021), a trend that tracks almost exactly with the increasing financialisation of the housing market. Mum’s point about essential workers being priced out of the communities they serve is also borne out: over 50% of low-income renters, a category that includes many nurses, teachers, and care workers, now spend more than 30% of their income on rent (AHURI, 2024).

Section 05: What Needs to Change

This is fixable.
But only if we act.

The data in this story isn't just numbers. It documents a market failure, a situation where the normal mechanisms of supply, demand and price have stopped delivering a basic necessity to the people who need it most.

When housing is primarily an investment asset, its price is driven by investor returns rather than community need. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: rising prices create wealth for those who already own property, while simultaneously making ownership less achievable for those who don't. This is not an accident; it is the predictable outcome of policy settings, particularly negative gearing and capital gains tax discounts, that systematically advantage investors over first home buyers. My mum identified this directly: housing should be a human right, not an opportunity for wealth accumulation by a few. The data backs that up. People own twenty properties while others can't afford one, and the tax system makes that arrangement profitable.

Addressing this requires treating housing the way we treat other essential infrastructure, as something government has a responsibility to ensure is accessible, not simply as a private market to be left alone. That means evaluating policies that distort the market in favour of investors, investing in supply, and accepting that intergenerational fairness is a legitimate goal of housing policy. Without that shift in how we think about what housing is for, the gap documented in this story will continue to widen with each generation.
